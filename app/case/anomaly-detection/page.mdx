## The problem

Maxon builds precision motors. The goal was to detect defective motors from their **current, voltage and temperature signals**. Two things made it hard:

- **Defects were rare.** Almost every motor was good, so the data was heavily imbalanced.
- **Factory noise masked them.** The signals came from a real production environment, where noise hides the small differences a defect causes.

## Choosing the metric

A defective motor that ships costs far more than a good motor flagged for a second check. So I **optimized for recall**: catching defects mattered more than avoiding false alarms.

## The benchmark

I compared two families of approaches on the same data:

- **Classical pipeline:** feature extraction from each signal channel, dimensionality reduction, then gradient boosting
- **Deep models:** an autoencoder and a Siamese network

**The autoencoder won.** It learns what a *normal* motor looks like and flags anything that doesn't fit, so it doesn't need many examples of defects. That made it a natural fit for imbalanced data.

## Shipping it

I took the autoencoder to production as an **inspection application integrated into the factory production line**:

- **FastAPI** service with **PostgreSQL**, packaged with **Docker**
- **JWT authentication** for secure access inside the factory
- **90%+ test coverage** (pytest and unittest), running in CI

I started on the ML side and then built the production software around the model, which is why my title covered both.

## Working with CSEM

I partnered with 2 AI experts from **CSEM** (the Swiss Center for Electronics and Microtechnology) and 3 engineers. My role was the link between the research side and the production software.

## What I learned

- **Pick the metric from the business cost.** Recall was the right target because a missed defect is the expensive mistake.
- **Benchmark before you commit.** Comparing classical and deep models on the same data gave us evidence for the final choice, not a guess.
- **With rare defects, model normal instead.** Learning what "good" looks like sidesteps the shortage of defect examples.
